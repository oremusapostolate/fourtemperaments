Philosophical & Classical Foundations

The doctrine of the four temperaments is one of the oldest instruments of self-knowledge in the West. It passed from the Greek physicians to the Scholastics, and from them into the classical manuals of ascetical theology and the practice of the confessional. Its purpose in Catholic life is not curiosity about personality, but the conquest of self and growth in virtue.

The Four Humors (Hippocrates & Galen)

The Hippocratic treatise On the Nature of Man taught that the body is governed by four humors; Galen (De temperamentis) linked each to two of the four primary qualities. A temperamentum is literally a “mixture” — the proportion in which these qualities are blended. Select a quadrant:

A word of honesty: modern physiology no longer accepts the humoral account of the body. The tradition — and Fr. The value lies in the observation of reactions, not in the old medicine.

The Thomistic & Ascetical Synthesis

St. Thomas Aquinas

St. Thomas teaches that the bodily disposition inclines a man to certain passions — the choleric, for instance, is “most quickly provoked to anger” (ST I-II, q.46, a.5) — and that from the temperament of the body some men have a natural aptitude toward certain virtues (I-II, q.63, a.1). Such aptitude is not yet virtue: virtue is a habit perfected by reason, free will and, in the supernatural order, by grace.

St. Teresa of Ávila

In the Book of the Foundations (ch. 7) the Saint gives prudent counsel to prioresses on governing sisters afflicted with “melancholy”: firmness joined to compassion, occupation, and refusal to let moods govern the community — an early model of temperament-aware direction.

St. Francis de Sales

The “Gentleman Saint” and Doctor of gentleness: a phlegmatic nature perfected by grace into unshakeable patience and calm, peaceable direction of souls. His Introduction to the Devout Life teaches meekness toward others and toward oneself — the constant remedy he prescribes to the impetuous and to the discouraged alike.

Fr. Conrad Hock & the Manuals

Fr. Hock’s little classic The Four Temperaments systematised the doctrine for priests, religious and educators: each temperament’s reaction pattern, its characteristic faults, and a method for its correction. Tanquerey’s The Spiritual Life likewise treats temperament within the knowledge of self required for spiritual direction.

Temperament is the raw material; character is what grace and the will make of it. No temperament is an excuse for sin, and none is an obstacle to sanctity.

Our Lord and Our Lady: the Perfect Harmony

The tradition holds that in Our Lord Jesus Christ, true God and true Man, and in His Immaculate Mother, the good qualities of all four temperaments are found in perfect balance and none of their defects: the zeal of the choleric without harshness (the cleansing of the Temple; the meekness of the Passion), the depth of the melancholic without despondency (the Agony in the Garden; the Magnificat kept and pondered in the heart), the warmth of the sanguine without levity (the wedding at Cana; the welcome of children), and the peace of the phlegmatic without sloth (the hidden life at Nazareth). They are the pattern toward which every temperament is to be conformed.

Right Use: Where, When & How

Where it is used

  • Self-knowledge in the daily examen and the particular examen.
  • Confession — to shape counsel and penance to the penitent.
  • Spiritual direction — to anticipate the soul’s typical temptations.
  • The family & the school — to form each child according to nature.
  • Community & parish life — to understand friction between persons.

When it is used

  • After observation over time, not at a first meeting.
  • When the same faults recur and a root needs naming.
  • When choosing a particular examen or a rule of life.
  • Not in moments of acute grief or crisis, when the soul needs comfort, not analysis.

How it is used

  • As a working hypothesis, held humbly and revised.
  • Never as a label or excuse (“I’m choleric, that’s how I am”).
  • Distinguish temperament, sin, and illness; refer to competent help where needed.
  • Attack the predominant fault; cultivate the opposite virtue.
  • Build on natural strengths — grace perfects nature.
For clarity this guide studies each temperament in its pure form. Each real person is to be known individually; the types are lenses for seeing, not boxes for enclosing.

Line of the Tradition

  • Hippocrates (c. 460–370 BC) & the Hippocratic school — the four humors.
  • Galen (AD 129–c. 216) — De temperamentis; humors linked to the primary qualities.
  • St. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) — bodily disposition, the passions, and natural aptitude for virtue.
  • St. Teresa of Ávila (1515–1582) — governing the melancholy in religious life.
  • St. Francis de Sales (1567–1622) — the Gentleman Saint; doctrine of gentleness.
  • Adolphe Tanquerey (1854–1932) — temperament in The Spiritual Life.
  • Fr. Conrad Hock (20th c.) — The Four Temperaments, the standard pastoral handbook.

Saints & Statesmen

Classic exemplars of each temperament. Note that assigning a temperament to a historical person is always an interpretation drawn from biographies and spiritual writers, and authors sometimes disagree. The saints especially show what grace makes of nature: their sanctity often consisted precisely in overcoming the faults of their temperament.

Tailored Admonitions & Penances

Scrupulosity and illness. Persistent, tormenting scruples, intrusive thoughts or compulsive re-confession may indicate obsessive-compulsive disorder; deep, lasting sadness may be clinical depression. These are not temperaments and not sins. Continue to give firm, simple direction, and gently encourage the penitent to consult a competent physician or a Catholic-minded mental-health professional alongside the sacraments.

B. Training of Virtues in Children & Youth

Following Fr. Hock and classical Catholic pedagogy: the educator does not try to change the child’s temperament but to form it — strengthening what is good and patiently pruning what is disordered.

General Principles for Parents & Teachers

  • Observe the child’s reactions for months before concluding; children change as they grow.
  • Correct the fault, never despise the temperament. Tell the child what is good in his nature.
  • Your own temperament affects how you correct: a choleric parent must guard against harshness; a phlegmatic parent against permissiveness.
  • Form habits through small, daily, repeated acts — virtue is built, not commanded.
  • Root all formation in prayer, the sacraments, and the example of the saints.
